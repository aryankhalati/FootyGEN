# Fouls and Cards

## Summary
Fouls and misconduct are covered by Law 12 of the Laws of the Game. A foul is an offence by a player against an opponent that the referee punishes with a free kick or a penalty kick. Misconduct is behaviour that can lead to a yellow card, which is a caution, or a red card, which is a sending-off. The referee judges each challenge as careless, reckless or using excessive force, and this decides whether a card is shown. Handball is also part of Law 12 and is covered in the handball doc.

## What is a foul?
A foul is an offence committed by a player against an opponent while the ball is in play. The main examples are kicking or trying to kick, tripping, jumping at, charging, striking, pushing and tackling or challenging an opponent. Holding, spitting and biting are also fouls. These offences are usually punished with a direct free kick. A foul needs contact in most cases, but some offences, such as dangerous play, can be punished without it.

## What do careless, reckless and excessive force mean?
Careless means the player showed a lack of attention or consideration, or acted without precaution. It is a foul, but it does not lead to a card. Reckless means the player acted with no regard for the danger to an opponent, or for the consequences. It is punished with a yellow card. Excessive force means the player used far more force than needed and put the opponent's safety at risk. It is punished with a red card.

## What is the restart for a foul?
A direct free kick is given for fouls such as kicking, tripping, pushing and handball. If the offender commits a direct free kick offence inside their own penalty area, the restart is a penalty kick instead. An indirect free kick is given for other offences, such as dangerous play, impeding an opponent without contact, dissent, and stopping the goalkeeper from releasing the ball. A goal cannot be scored directly from an indirect free kick.

## What gets a yellow card?
A yellow card is a caution. The main reasons are unsporting behaviour, dissent, persistent offences, delaying the restart of play, failing to retreat the required distance at a restart, and entering or leaving the field without the referee's permission. Unsporting behaviour includes a reckless foul, a foul to stop a promising attack, handball to stop an attack, and simulation, such as pretending to be fouled. A player who gets a second yellow card in the same match is sent off with a red card.

## What gets a red card?
A red card is a sending-off. The reasons are serious foul play, violent conduct, spitting at or biting someone, denying a goal or an obvious goal-scoring opportunity by a foul or handball, using offensive, insulting or abusive language or gestures, and receiving a second yellow card in the same match. Serious foul play is a challenge that uses excessive force or puts an opponent's safety at risk. A sent-off player must leave the field and cannot be replaced, so the team plays with one player fewer. Competition rules decide any suspension.

## What is denying an obvious goal-scoring opportunity?
Denying an obvious goal-scoring opportunity is often shortened to DOGSO. The referee considers the distance between the offence and the goal, the general direction of play, the likelihood of keeping or gaining control of the ball, and the location and number of defenders and attackers. If the offence is a foul that stops the opportunity, the player is normally sent off. If it happens in the offender's penalty area and the player tried to play the ball, the punishment is a penalty kick and a yellow card. Holding, pulling, pushing or serious foul play still gets a red card.

## What is advantage?
The referee can let play continue after a foul if the team that was fouled can benefit. This is called advantage. If the benefit does not appear within a few seconds, the referee can go back and give the free kick. The referee can still show a card for the offence at the next stoppage. Since the 2026/27 Laws took effect on 1 July 2026, a player is not cautioned for a DOGSO offence if the referee plays advantage and a goal is scored.

## What changed for cards in the 2026/27 Laws?
The 2026/27 Laws took effect on 1 July 2026. VAR can now review a red card that came from a clearly incorrect second yellow card. It can also correct mistaken identity, when a yellow or red card is shown to the wrong player. For a yellow card that is not a second yellow, VAR can only identify the right player, not review the offence. Attackers are now one of the factors the referee considers for DOGSO. Competitions can also choose to give a red card to a player who covers their mouth to hide abuse aimed at an opponent.
