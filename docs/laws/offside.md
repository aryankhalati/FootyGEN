# Offside Rule

## Summary
Offside is covered by Law 11 of the Laws of the Game. It stops attackers from waiting near the opponents' goal for the ball. A player is penalised only if they are in an offside position when a teammate plays the ball and then become involved in active play. Being in an offside position is not an offence by itself. The punishment is an indirect free kick to the other team.

## What is an offside position?
A player is in an offside position if they are in the opponents' half and any part of their head, body or feet is nearer to the opponents' goal line than both the ball and the second-last opponent. The goalkeeper is often the last opponent, so the second-last is often the last outfield defender, but the rule applies to any two opponents. Hands and arms do not count, for any player. The position is judged at the moment a teammate plays or touches the ball, not when the attacker receives it.

## When is offside an offence?
A player in an offside position is penalised only if they become involved in active play. That means interfering with play by touching a ball passed or touched by a teammate, interfering with an opponent, or gaining an advantage from their position. Interfering with an opponent includes blocking their line of vision, challenging them for the ball, or making an obvious action that affects their ability to play it. A player who stays out of the play and does not affect an opponent is not penalised.

## Where does offside not apply?
A player cannot be offside in their own half. A player level with the second-last opponent, or level with the last two opponents, is onside. No offside offence can be committed directly from a goal kick, a throw-in or a corner kick. These are the most common edge cases and are often misjudged on a quick viewing.

## Deliberate play, deflections and rebounds
If a defender deliberately plays the ball, an attacker who was in an offside position and receives it is not penalised. A deflection or rebound is different, and does not count as a deliberate play. If the ball rebounds or is deflected off a goalpost, the crossbar or a match official, an attacker in an offside position who then plays it can still be penalised for gaining an advantage. The same applies to a ball that comes from a deliberate save by an opponent, because a deliberate save does not reset offside.

## What happens after an offside offence?
The referee awards an indirect free kick to the opposing team, taken from the place where the offending player was when they became involved in active play. This applies even if that place is in the offending player's own half, which can happen when a player who was offside when the ball was played later goes back to touch it.
