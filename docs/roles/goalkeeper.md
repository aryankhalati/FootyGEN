# Goalkeeper and the Sweeper-Keeper

## Summary
The goalkeeper is the only player allowed to handle the ball inside their own penalty area, and wears a kit that is different from teammates and opponents. The main jobs are stopping shots, protecting the goal and organising the defence. Modern goalkeepers are also expected to play with their feet and help the team build attacks. A sweeper-keeper is a goalkeeper who also defends space well outside the penalty area, usually behind a high defensive line.

## What does a goalkeeper do?
A goalkeeper stops shots, claims or punches crosses, and deals with one-on-one situations. They also act as the last line of defence, so they see the whole pitch and tell defenders when to push up, hold their positions or mark an opponent. Good communication matters as much as saves. A goalkeeper who commands the penalty area gives the defenders confidence to defend further from goal.

## How do the rules treat a goalkeeper?
Inside their own penalty area, a goalkeeper may pick up the ball. They cannot handle it after a teammate deliberately kicks it to them, or after a throw-in from a teammate. The punishment is an indirect free kick. A goalkeeper also cannot control the ball with their hands or arms for more than eight seconds. The referee shows a visual five-second countdown, and if the goalkeeper still has the ball when it ends, the opponents are awarded a corner kick. Outside their own penalty area, a goalkeeper is treated like any other player, so handling the ball there is handball. The handball doc covers this in more detail.

## Shot-stopping and positioning
Shot-stopping depends on position as well as reflexes. A goalkeeper stands where they can cover the most of the goal, and moves with the ball. Coming off the line narrows the angle for an attacker, which makes it harder to shoot. In a one-on-one, a goalkeeper tries to stay big and balanced and delay a decision for as long as possible. Staying set when the shot is taken helps them react.

## Distribution and build-up
Distribution means how the goalkeeper restarts play. Options include throwing to a full-back, playing a short pass to a centre-back, or kicking long to a forward or winger. In a 4-3-3 that builds from the back, the goalkeeper acts as an extra player in the build-up. Their passing can draw the opposition press forward, which creates space for a free player beyond it. Weak passing under pressure can give away the ball in a dangerous area.

## What is a sweeper-keeper?
A sweeper-keeper plays high, often near the edge of the penalty area or beyond it, and covers the space behind the defensive line. Through balls that would otherwise reach a forward can be cleared by the goalkeeper before the forward arrives. This fits a team that defends with a high line or presses high. A sweeper-keeper is usually also comfortable with the ball at their feet. Manuel Neuer is often cited as an example.

## What are the risks of the sweeper-keeper style?
Standing high leaves the goal exposed to long shots and lobs. The goalkeeper must judge when to come out and when to stay, and a late or wrong decision can lead to a goal or a sending off. Outside the area they cannot use their hands, so they must clear with their feet or head. The style also depends on the team. If the defensive line is not high and compact, the sweeper-keeper is more exposed.

## What qualities does a goalkeeper need?
The role needs quick reflexes and good positioning, because most saves depend on being in the right place before the shot is taken. Handling, command of the box on crosses and calm decisions in one-on-one situations matter most. A goalkeeper also needs strong communication to organise the defence, and composure with the ball at their feet, since modern teams use the goalkeeper in build-up. Scanning before receiving a pass helps the goalkeeper choose a safe option or a forward one under pressure.
