# Winger and Inverted Winger Roles

## Summary
A winger is an attacking player who plays wide on the left or right. In a 4-3-3 the two wingers are part of the front three, next to the central striker. Wingers give the team width, take on defenders and attack space behind the defence. The two main types are the traditional winger, who stays wide, and the inverted winger, who cuts inside towards goal.

## What does a traditional winger do?
A traditional winger stays near the touchline and tries to beat the opposing full-back by dribbling or by speed. The aim is to reach the byline or a crossing position and deliver the ball into the box. Staying wide also stretches the defence and creates space in the centre for the midfielders and striker. Traditional wingers are usually positioned on the same side as their stronger foot.

## What is an inverted winger?
An inverted winger plays on the side opposite their stronger foot, for example a right-footed player on the left. This makes it natural to cut inside onto the stronger foot, which brings the player closer to goal. From there the winger can shoot, play a pass into the box, or combine with a teammate. The role is also called an inside forward.

## How does the winger affect the full-back?
The winger's movement decides what the full-back on that side does. If the winger cuts inside, the full-back usually supplies the width, often by overlapping on the outside. If the winger stays wide, the full-back can move inside, either by underlapping or as an inverted full-back. A team can use a different solution on each flank.

## What does a winger do without the ball?
Without the ball, the winger presses from the front or drops back to help the midfield. When the wingers drop back alongside the midfield, a 4-3-3 becomes a 4-5-1 that protects the flanks. Pressing works only if the whole team moves together. A winger who presses alone lets the opponent play around them.

## Which type of winger is better?
Neither is better in every case. A traditional winger gives natural width and suits a team that crosses the ball or wants to isolate a full-back one-on-one. An inverted winger suits a team that wants more shots and combinations near the box. Against a crowded centre, a winger who stays wide and receives a quick switch of play can create a one-versus-one. Mohamed Salah is often cited as an example of an inverted winger.

## What are the risks?
Wingers can be isolated if the team does not support them, which can lead to too many crosses or individual dribbling. An inverted winger who cuts inside every time can become predictable, and the team may lose width if the full-back does not move wide.

## What qualities does a winger need?
The role needs pace and dribbling ability, because the winger often has to beat a full-back one-on-one. A traditional winger also needs accurate crossing, while an inverted winger needs good shooting and finishing, plus passing to combine near the box. Well-timed runs in behind the defence matter for both types. A winger also needs the work rate to press and track back when the team loses the ball. Scanning before receiving helps the winger choose between taking on the defender, cutting inside or passing.
