# 4-2-3-1 Formation

## Summary
The 4-2-3-1 is a formation with four defenders, two holding midfielders, three attacking midfielders and one striker. The defence is two centre-backs and two full-backs, the two holding midfielders form a double pivot, and the attacking line is a left winger, a central attacking midfielder (the No. 10) and a right winger behind a lone striker. The numbers describe the starting shape, not where players stand in every phase. In possession it often becomes a 3-2-5 type structure, and without the ball it usually drops into a 4-4-1-1 or 4-4-2. It balances defensive security with creativity in central areas.

## Shape and roles
In the 4-2-3-1, the two holding midfielders sit in front of the back four and protect it. The No. 10 plays between the opposition's midfield and defence, linking the double pivot and the striker. The two wide players provide width, take on full-backs and cut inside to shoot or combine. The lone striker either holds the ball up, runs in behind or drops to link play. The full-backs defend the flanks and give width when the wide players move inside. The 4-2-3-1 does not require the two holding midfielders or the three attackers to have identical jobs.

## Double pivot variations
The two holding midfielders in the 4-2-3-1 can be paired in different ways. In a defender and passer pairing, one player screens the back four and wins the ball while the other dictates play with his passing. In a balanced pairing, both can defend and carry the ball, and they take turns stepping forward. In a box-to-box pairing, one midfielder joins the attack more often, while the other stays back as cover. The pivot is the foundation of the formation. If both players push forward at once, the back four is left exposed, so they normally coordinate who advances.

## In possession: build-up
The 4-2-3-1 builds from the goalkeeper and centre-backs, with the double pivot offering short passing options in front of them. One of the pivot can drop between or beside the centre-backs to help build, which lets the full-backs push higher. If the pivot is pressed, the team can play to the full-backs or go direct to the lone striker. The aim of build-up is to draw the opposition forward and play through the middle to the No. 10 or the wide players. Having two players in the pivot gives the team a secure base to recycle possession.

## In possession: wingers and full-backs
In the 4-2-3-1, the wide attackers either stay wide or cut inside. When a winger stays wide, he stretches the opposition and creates one-versus-one situations, and the full-back can underlap or stay back. When a winger cuts inside, he joins the No. 10 in the central areas and the full-back overlaps to supply the width. Inverted wingers, such as a right-footer on the left, often cut inside to shoot. The full-backs are important to the formation's width, so teams must choose which full-back attacks and which stays back for cover.

## In possession: the lone striker
The lone striker in a 4-2-3-1 can play several roles. A target man holds the ball up and brings the No. 10 and wingers into play. A mobile striker runs in behind and stretches the defence. A false nine drops into midfield to receive the ball, which lets the No. 10 and wingers attack the space left behind. A striker who works alone must be supported by quick arrivals from the three behind him. If the support is slow, the striker becomes isolated and the team loses its link to the final third.

## In possession: final third
The 4-2-3-1 creates chances through the No. 10's passes between the lines, wingers cutting inside to shoot, overlapping full-backs, crosses and through balls to the striker. With four players in advanced positions, the team has many players around the penalty area. The No. 10 can pass into the striker, play wide, or shoot from the edge of the box. Against a compact defence, quick combinations between the No. 10 and wingers can open space. A double pivot player can also arrive late at the edge of the area for a shot or a cut-back.

## Rest defence
The 4-2-3-1 has natural rest defence because the double pivot already sits in front of the centre-backs. If the full-backs go forward, the two centre-backs and the pivot can still form a solid central block of four against a counter-attack. The key is that at least one pivot player stays back when the other advances. Rest defence is weaker when both full-backs and both pivot players join the attack together, because the centre-backs are then left with a lot of space to cover.

## Out of possession: pressing
The 4-2-3-1 presses with the striker and the No. 10 leading, the wingers closing the full-backs and the double pivot following behind. In a high press, the formation often turns into a 4-4-1-1 or 4-4-2, with the No. 10 stepping up beside the striker. The pivot moves up to take the central passing options and the wingers press the full-backs. Pressing works only if the whole team moves together, usually on triggers such as a backward pass or a poor touch. A winger who presses alone leaves the full-back free.

## Out of possession: defensive blocks
The 4-2-3-1 defends in a mid-block or low block, usually as a 4-4-1-1. The wingers drop back alongside the pivot to form a flat midfield four, and the No. 10 sits just behind the striker. The double pivot protects the centre, and this makes it hard for opponents to play through the middle. The No. 10 can screen the opposition's holding midfielder or press centre-backs. Teams often choose between pressing high and defending in a block depending on the opponent and the match.

## Transitions
When a 4-2-3-1 wins the ball, the No. 10 and wingers break forward quickly while the striker runs in behind or holds the ball up. The double pivot gives a safe outlet to calm play if there is no forward option. When it loses the ball, the team can counter-press with the nearby attackers, or drop into its defensive shape. The pivot is the key to defensive transition because it can cut off the first counter-attacking pass before the back four is stretched.

## Strengths
The 4-2-3-1 gives defensive security through its double pivot, with two players protecting the back four. It has many players in central areas, so the team can overload the middle and create passing options. The No. 10 gives a clear creative link between midfield and attack. The wingers give width and goal threat. It is also flexible, because small changes to roles can turn it into a 4-4-2 or 4-3-3 without changing the nominal shape.

## Weaknesses
The main weakness of the 4-2-3-1 is the lone striker, who can be isolated by two centre-backs if the support is slow. The formation relies heavily on the quality of the No. 10, and if he is marked out of the game the team can lose its creativity. The wide players must work hard both ways, and the full-backs are exposed if they do not track back. The double pivot can also be outnumbered by a three-man midfield if the attackers do not help in the centre.

## Related shapes
The 4-2-3-1 is closely related to other formations. The 4-4-1-1 is usually its defensive version, with the wingers dropping into a flat midfield four. The 4-3-3 is similar, but has one holding midfielder and three forwards instead of a double pivot and a No. 10. A 4-3-3 with two deeper midfielders can resemble it during play. The 4-4-2 can also be a variation, if the No. 10 plays beside the striker. A label can refer to the starting formation, the attacking structure or the defensive block, and these can differ within one match.

## Example: Heynckes' Bayern Munich
Jupp Heynckes' Bayern Munich won the treble in 2012-13, winning the Bundesliga, the DFB-Pokal and the Champions League, mostly with a 4-2-3-1. Bastian Schweinsteiger and Javi Martínez formed the double pivot, with Thomas Müller as the No. 10 and Arjen Robben and Franck Ribéry wide. Mario Mandžukić led the line as a physical striker who pressed and held the ball up. Bayern pressed aggressively, used the double pivot to control the middle, and attacked through the wide players and Müller's movement. David Alaba and Philipp Lahm gave width from full-back.

## Example: Mourinho's Real Madrid
José Mourinho's Real Madrid used a 4-2-3-1 in 2011-12, when they won La Liga with a record 100 points. Xabi Alonso and Sami Khedira formed the double pivot, with Mesut Özil as the No. 10. Cristiano Ronaldo and Ángel Di María played wide, and Karim Benzema was the lone striker. Madrid were known for fast counter-attacks, with the pivot winning the ball and Alonso's long passes releasing the wide players in behind. The team could also press high and attack in transition, which made it hard to defend against at pace.
