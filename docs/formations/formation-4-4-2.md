# 4-4-2 Formation

## Summary
The 4-4-2 is a formation with four defenders, four midfielders and two strikers. The defence is two centre-backs and two full-backs, the midfield is two central midfielders and two wide midfielders, and the attack is a pair of strikers. The numbers describe the starting shape, not where players stand in every phase. In possession it can stretch into a 4-2-4 shape with the wide midfielders pushing high, and without the ball it defends in two banks of four. It is known for simplicity, balance and compactness, and teams use it for direct play, crossing, pressing and counter-attacks.

## Shape and roles
In the 4-4-2, the two central midfielders sit side by side in the middle. One usually holds and protects the back four while the other supports the attack, though some pairs share both jobs. The two wide midfielders provide width, deliver crosses and track back to help the full-backs. The full-backs defend the flanks and overlap behind the wide midfielders. The two strikers work as a partnership, pinning the centre-backs and attacking the box. The 4-4-2 relies on every player knowing his job inside a clear, simple structure.

## Striker partnerships
The 4-4-2 depends on how its two strikers fit together. A common pairing is a target man, who holds the ball up and wins aerial duels, with a runner who attacks space in behind. Another pairing is a poacher who finishes chances with a creator who drops deeper to link play, sometimes called a second striker. Two mobile strikers can also swap roles and drag centre-backs out of position. A good partnership gives the team a clear outlet, and a poor one leaves two forwards doing the same job in the same space.

## In possession: build-up
The 4-4-2 builds from the goalkeeper and centre-backs, but it is also comfortable going longer. With only two central midfielders, one often drops towards the centre-backs to receive the ball, while the other stays higher. A long pass to a striker's chest or into the channels is a normal option, not a last resort. The strikers drop to receive short passes or stay high to stretch the opposition line. Against a high press, the team can bypass it by going long and winning the second ball with the central midfielders and wide players.

## In possession: wide midfielders and full-backs
In the 4-4-2, the wide midfielders and full-backs create most of the team's width. An overlapping full-back runs outside the wide midfielder, which can create a two-versus-one against the opposing full-back. If a wide midfielder cuts inside, the full-back supplies the width. Wide players deliver crosses early, from deep or near the byline, and the strikers attack the near and far posts. Crossing is one of the signature routes of the 4-4-2.

## In possession: the strikers
The two strikers in the 4-4-2 divide the work in front of goal. One attacks the front post or the near channel while the other attacks the far post or the penalty spot. When the ball is in midfield, one striker can drop to link play while the other runs in behind, and this stops both forwards from being marked by the same centre-back. The strikers also press from the front and force defenders into long passes. A team can use the two forwards to win aerial duels, hold the ball up or run into the channels, depending on the profiles.

## In possession: final third
The 4-4-2 creates chances through crosses, through balls, quick one-twos between the strikers, and runs from wide midfielders into the box. The team often has three or four players in or around the penalty area when a cross arrives, which makes it dangerous from wide positions. Central midfielders arrive late at the edge of the box for cut-backs and shots. Against a deep defence, the team can use wide play and crosses. Against a high line, a pass in behind for a striker or a wide midfielder is often worth more than slow combinations.

## Rest defence
When a 4-4-2 attacks, it must keep enough players back to stop a counter-attack. Rest defence here usually means the two centre-backs plus one central midfielder, with the other midfielder and full-backs free to join the attack. If both full-backs and both wide midfielders go forward, the team is exposed to counters down the flanks. The holding central midfielder is the key protector, and he has to cover the space between the centre-backs and the advancing players.

## Out of possession: pressing
The 4-4-2 presses with its two strikers on the opposition centre-backs and the goalkeeper, while the midfield four stay close behind. One striker closes the ball carrier and the other blocks the pass to the nearest central midfielder, often called curving the run. The midfield then moves up as a unit to squeeze the pitch. Pressing in a 4-4-2 works best when the lines stay close together and the team moves on clear triggers, such as a poor touch or a pass to a full-back. A striker who presses alone leaves a gap between the forwards and the midfield.

## Out of possession: defensive blocks
The 4-4-2 is best known for its compact mid-block and low block. The team defends in two banks of four, with a bank of four defenders and a bank of four midfielders, and the two strikers stand just ahead of them. The gap between the banks is small, so there is little space between the lines for the opponent to receive the ball. The wide midfielders tuck in to protect the middle and drop back to help the full-backs against wingers. The two strikers can drop to cut passing lanes into the opposing midfield or stay high as an outlet for counter-attacks.

## Transitions
When a 4-4-2 wins the ball, it attacks quickly. A central midfielder or wide player plays a direct pass to a striker, and the other striker and the wide midfielders run beyond him. The two strikers give the team two outlets, which makes counter-attacks simple and fast. When the team loses the ball, it can counter-press in the middle or drop straight back into its two banks of four. The shape is easy to recover into, which is one reason it is hard to counter against.

## Strengths
The 4-4-2 is simple, balanced and easy to understand, so players know their jobs clearly. Its two banks of four make it hard to play through in the central areas. Two strikers give the team a constant outlet and two forwards to attack crosses. It has natural width through the wide midfielders and full-backs, and it transitions quickly from defence to attack. It also uses its forwards to press the opposition's centre-backs.

## Weaknesses
The main weakness of the 4-4-2 is the central midfield, where only two players face a three-man midfield in formations like the 4-3-3 or 4-2-3-1. The opposition can overload the middle and find a free player between the lines. Wide midfielders must work hard both ways, and a tired wide midfielder leaves the full-back exposed. The team can lose control of possession against a side with better ball-players in the middle. Two strikers who are too similar can also leave the team with no link between midfield and attack.

## Related shapes
The 4-4-2 comes in several versions. The 4-4-2 diamond (or 4-1-2-1-2) keeps two strikers but uses a narrow midfield with a holding player, two central players and an attacking midfielder, and relies on full-backs for width. The 4-4-1-1 drops one striker slightly behind the other, which adds a link player. The 4-2-3-1 is closely related, because its two holding midfielders and wingers can look like a 4-4-2 in defence. A label can describe the starting shape or the defensive block, and these can differ.

## Example: Ferguson's Manchester United
Sir Alex Ferguson's Manchester United used a 4-4-2 for much of their 1998-99 treble-winning season. Beckham on the right and Giggs on the left provided width and crosses, and Roy Keane and Paul Scholes formed the central midfield pair in many big games, though both were suspended for the Champions League final. Dwight Yorke and Andy Cole were the main strike partnership, combining movement and finishing. United attacked quickly down both flanks with crosses and fast combinations. Ferguson had strong depth in midfield and attack, so he changed the personnel from match to match.

## Example: Ranieri's Leicester City
Claudio Ranieri's Leicester City won the 2015-16 Premier League mainly with a 4-4-2. Jamie Vardy and Shinji Okazaki were the usual strikers, with Vardy running in behind and Okazaki pressing and linking play. Riyad Mahrez and Marc Albrighton played wide, and N'Golo Kanté and Danny Drinkwater formed the central pair. Leicester defended in two compact banks of four and attacked quickly on the counter, using direct passes into Vardy's runs. They often had less possession than their opponents, which shows that the formation can win without dominating the ball.
